Ledger: worth it the moment self-custody matters

If you're holding crypto anywhere other than an exchange's own custody, the question isn't whether to use a hardware wallet — it's which one. Here's what Ledger actually gets you, and where it stops mattering.

Hardware wallet One-time purchase $79–$399
Verdict

Buy the Nano S Plus if you just need your private keys off your laptop. Buy the Stax or Flex only if the touchscreen and clear-signing display are worth the extra cost to you — for most solo builders, they're a comfort upgrade, not a security one.

What it's actually for

A Ledger device keeps your private keys on a separate, offline chip instead of on whatever computer you happen to be using. The practical effect: even if your laptop is compromised — a bad browser extension, a malicious dependency in a project you cloned, a phishing page that looks exactly like a real dApp — a transaction still has to be physically confirmed on the device itself. That single step is what stops most wallet-drain attacks, which rely on getting a signature without you noticing.

For a solo builder specifically, this matters more than it might for someone with a security team reviewing what gets installed on company machines. You're the only line of defense on your own hardware, and you're also the person most likely to be testing unfamiliar packages, connecting to unfamiliar contracts, and moving quickly. That combination is exactly the profile phishing kits are built for.

Where it holds up

  • Private keys never touch an internet-connected device
  • Physical confirmation blocks most remote drain attacks
  • Works across most major chains and wallets (MetaMask, Rabby, etc.)
  • Open-source firmware review process, not fully closed

Where it doesn't

  • Doesn't protect you if you approve a malicious contract yourself
  • Ledger Live's past data-breach history (2020) is worth knowing about — it affected customer contact info, not funds
  • Seed phrase storage is still entirely on you
  • Stax/Flex premium buys convenience, not materially more security

Who should skip this: if everything you hold lives on an exchange and you're comfortable with that custody model, a hardware wallet solves a problem you don't have yet. It earns its cost the day you move funds into your own wallet.
